HOW IT WORKS


  
What this actually does

  
Nobody knows what your players will score on Sunday. So rather than guess once, the machine plays your matchup 10,000 times — 10,000 alternate Sundays, each one a little different — and counts how often you win.

  
That’s where your win odds come from. Not a hunch. A tally.


  
Why it sometimes says “coin flip”

  
Every number here comes with a margin for error. When two lineup choices are closer together than that margin, the machine says so instead of picking one anyway.

  
That’s deliberate. A tool that always sounds confident is bluffing some of the time. This one would rather tell you the truth: it genuinely doesn’t matter — go with whoever’s injury report you like better.


  
Why it won’t guess your roster

  
Players get traded. A tool that “remembers” rosters goes stale the day someone changes teams, and you’d never know — it would quietly simulate the wrong guy on the wrong team and hand you a confident answer.

  
So the machine knows nothing on its own. Every name, team and number comes from the data in front of you. If something’s wrong you can see it, and fix it in one tap.


  
The small print under your results

  
That last line tallies where each projection came from. consensus came straight from a public projection table. derived was worked out from a season-long number. ESTIMATE-UNVERIFIED means nobody published one and it’s a best guess — treat those with suspicion. edited means you typed it yourself.

  
It’s there so the results never look more solid than the numbers underneath them.


  
Floor and ceiling

  
Every player row shows a range like 9–28. That’s his bad day to his best day: across those 10,000 Sundays he lands somewhere in that range about 8 times out of 10. The single projection is just the middle of it.

  
Two players can share a projection and have completely different ranges. Quite often, that is the start/sit decision.


  
For the nerds 🤓

  
It’s a Monte Carlo simulation. Skill-position scores are drawn from a lognormal matched to each player’s projection and standard deviation — right-skewed, floored at zero, because a receiver can drop 40 on you but never −3. Kickers and defenses use a normal with a hard floor.

  
Every player gets his own fixed random stream, so when a bench swap is tested the other eight starters are bit-identical week to week. That makes it a paired comparison, which shrinks the error bar on a swap by about 6× against two independent runs. The quoted bar is two standard errors of that paired difference, and it has been checked against the estimator’s real spread — claimed over true comes out at 0.99.

  
Floor and ceiling are the 10th and 90th percentile of each player’s own draws, taken by quickselect rather than a full sort.

  
There is also a command-line version in the repo. It uses a different random number generator, so the same seed produces different draws — same statistics, different digits. Both converge on the same answer.

  
The full spec — including a candid account of what the model gets wrong and what evidence would overturn each design choice — is in ENGINE.md. Source on GitHub.
